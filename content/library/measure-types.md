A project needs more than one measure because a single number cannot tell you
whether you improved the thing you meant to improve, whether you actually did
what you said you would do, or whether you broke something else on the way.

## The three types

**Outcome measures** answer: did the thing we care about get better? They are
the reason the project exists — mortality, readmission, time to antibiotics,
patient-reported experience. They move slowly, they are affected by much more
than your intervention, and they are usually the measure a program director
asks about first.

**Process measures** answer: did we actually do what we said we would do? They
sit closer to the team's own behaviour — proportion of handoffs using the
template, proportion of orders placed through the new pathway. They move
quickly, which makes them the measures that tell you within a week whether a
PDSA cycle happened at all. A project whose process measures are flat has not
been implemented, whatever the outcome measure is doing.

**Balancing measures** answer: what did we break? Reducing length of stay may
raise readmissions. Speeding triage may raise mis-triage. Adding a checklist
may add documentation time that comes out of direct patient care.

## Balancing measures are the ones that get omitted

This is the most commonly missing measure, and its absence is not neutral. A
project without a balancing measure can only produce good news, which is
exactly why reviewers distrust it. Intake blocks submission without one, and
the block is not a formality — if you cannot name a plausible harm, you have
probably not understood the system you are changing.

Ask: if this intervention worked perfectly, and someone were harmed by it, how
would that harm show up, and in what number?

## Operational definitions

A measure is not defined until every one of these is written down:

- **Numerator** — what counts as an event.
- **Denominator** — the population at risk of being counted.
- **Inclusions** — which cases belong in the denominator.
- **Exclusions** — which cases are removed, and why.
- **Data source** — the system or report the numbers come from.
- **Puller** — the named person or team who extracts it.
- **Cadence** — how often it is pulled.

The test of an operational definition is reproducibility: two people applying
it to the same period must get the same number. The definition builder refuses
to save until all seven fields are present, then restates the definition as a
plain-language data query so you can confirm it describes what you meant.

## Shared definitions

Where the institution has a library definition for a concept — readmission,
length of stay, left without being seen — use it rather than writing a local
one. Programs defining "readmission" five different ways is how an institution
loses the ability to compare anything. Local definitions can be promoted to the
library by the chair once they have proven themselves.

When a shared definition changes, every chart built on the old one changes
meaning. Superseded definitions are therefore never edited in place: a new
version is created, and charts built on the old one carry a banner naming the
change and its date.
